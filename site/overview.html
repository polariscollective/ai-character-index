What each constitution says, and how well it is built

AI companies publish constitutions: documents setting out how their models should* behave. Each company has its own name for its document: OpenAI calls its one the Model Spec, Anthropic calls its one Claude’s Constitution. We call them all constitutions, for the reasons the About page gives. We assess two aspects of each one: the document as a whole, meaning how consistent and coherent it is, and how well it addresses each desirable model behaviour. A company that publishes none scores 0.

Missing a constitution, or a behaviour that this index should be assessing? Propose one.

    Notes

    1. Final score. The average of the document as a whole and the behaviours, each counting for half. It ranks the companies. Every figure on the board is out of 10, and each row says under its name how much it counts for in the figure it belongs to.
    2. The document as a whole. Whether the constitution settles a clash between two of its own rules, whether a reader can tell which rules are absolute and which can be lifted and by whom, whether the rules come with reasons, whether they reach the situations the models are used in, and whether the document contradicts itself somewhere without saying which rule wins.
    3. The behaviours. How far the constitution goes on each behaviour the index asks about, from saying nothing about it to setting rules and showing them applied to worked examples, on the scale above. For us, a behaviour is one precise thing a constitution can ask of a model, such as deferring to users’ decisions about their own lives, or not asserting what the model believes to be false. Each is written in two parts, what it requires and where it stops, so that a careful reader could mark every passage of a constitution that bears on it. In the doc reader you can pick a behaviour and read every passage of a constitution that bears on it.
    4. Categories. For now the behaviours are grouped into categories. We wrote the behaviours, their definitions and the categories ourselves, and all three are open to challenge.

    Takeaways

    Motivation

    A constitution is the document in which a company says how its models should behave, and it is the only public statement against which a model’s behaviour can be checked. Its defaults shape what hundreds of millions of people come to expect of an AI system.

    Nobody can program a model to follow it. A model is trained on examples, so the rules have to be written precisely before anyone can train a model on them or test it against them. This board reads each constitution for that precision, as a document and behaviour by behaviour.

    What comes next

    New constitutions, and new versions of the ones already read, will be read as they are published, and readers can propose both. The behaviours, their definitions and their categories will change as they are challenged.

    The next view is adherence: measuring how closely each company’s models follow the constitution it published. That is what ties the quality of a document to the behaviour of the models it governs.

    How constitutions are governed

    Some AI companies publish a constitution, a document setting out how their models should* behave. This view scores nine companies, as of September 2026, on two things: how transparent they are about the constitutions they publish, and the extent to which those constitutions are actually made to bind the models. The final score is the average of the two, and it ranks the companies.

    Takeaways

    Notes

    Motivation

    A constitution is the only public statement of how a company intends its models to behave, which makes it the reference against which any claim about a model’s behaviour can be checked. It can serve that purpose only if outsiders can see which models it governs and when it changes.

    As of September 2026 significant changes can happen out of sight: a model deployed under rules nobody has published, a guardrail tightened or loosened without notice, a rule weakened before anything is said. This board scores what each company makes visible. It asks for disclosure, and says nothing about how a company should decide what its rules are.

    Detailed scoring

    Where the rows come from

    The board has two sources and no others. The four questions are the four asks of Polaris Collective's working paper "Model spec governance and transparency", in its draft of 10 September 2026. The ten best practices beside them come from "Emerging International Best Practices for AI Model Specs", a working paper by Edward Kembery and colleagues that is under review. Every row on the board carries the number of the note that names its source, and the paper's own words sit in the popover the row's name opens. Neither paper splits a question into checks, weighs one row against another, or says what earns a score. We did all three, and every score on the board is ours.

    How a figure is worked out

    A question is split into checks, each scored from 0 to 4: three for each of the first two questions and two for the others. Every check carries a written description of what earns 0, what earns 2 and what earns 4, in the tables below. A question's own figure is the average of its checks. A best practice is scored 0, 1 or 2. Four of them carry a description of each score of their own, and the others share one scale.

    The board shows every score out of 10, as its share of the scale it was given on: a check scored 3 of 4 shows 7.5, and a practice scored 1 of 2 shows 5.0. The score as given is in the panel that opens on it.

    Each row belongs to one figure and one only, and every row of a figure counts the same. What is published has eleven rows, the ten checks and the licence practice, so each counts for 1/11 of it and a question with three checks counts for 3/11. What it engages has eight, all of them practices, each counting for 1/8. Every row says under its name how much it counts for.

    The final score averages the two figures, each counting for half, and companies are ranked on it. Companies level on it share a place. The two figures measure different things, what a company has published and what it says about its own practice, so both stay on the board under the final score and a reader can see which half a company earned it on.

    Below, every question, check and practice is named as it is on the board. The tables say what earns 0, 2 and 4 on each check, and a score of 1 or 3 falls between the descriptions either side of it.

    The best practices, figure by figure

    The ten best practices come from the second working paper named above, note 6 under the board. Each is named below as it is on the board, followed by what the paper asks and where it asks it.

    One practice is counted in what is published, because it is a fact about the published text.

      Four are counted in what it engages and can be checked by anyone from public sources.

        Four more are counted in the same figure, and nobody outside a company can see whether it follows them. The paper asks companies to say so publicly, so each is scored 0, 1 or 2 on what the company publishes, and 0 when it publishes nothing.

          One is scored for nobody, for the reason note 7 gives, and is counted in neither figure.

            The paper gives no description of what 0, 1 and 2 mean on a practice. In our scoring, 2 is the practice in full, 1 part of it or a weaker form of it, and 0 means we found nothing published.

            How we looked

            Every score was given by hand, by reading documents. No model judged any row, and no score was taken from another ranking. The reading was done between 18 and 21 September 2026, and the sources it rests on are listed company by company below.

            For each company we searched its own pages first: the constitution or policy document where one exists, the developer documentation, the model cards and system cards, the terms of service and usage policy, the change logs and release notes, and the safety or frontier framework. We then searched for the company by name against the practices it had not answered, and read the outside sources listed below, among them the EU Code of Practice signatory list, the Stanford Foundation Model Transparency Index, and the evaluations published by the UK AI Security Institute and the US Center for AI Standards and Innovation. For the four practices only a company can show, the search was run one practice at a time across all nine companies, so that each practice was judged the same way everywhere.

            Every row was read again on 23 and 24 September 2026, and the passage behind each score was recorded with its address and its date.

            Where nothing is published, the board says nothing is published. A 0 is not a judgement that a company does not do something. It says we looked and found nothing public, and a company may well follow a practice without saying so. Every row on the board rests on a passage quoted with its address in the popover behind the score, or on a sentence there saying where we looked and found nothing. Two searches failed outright, and both are named under Limitations: the Chinese regulatory filings, and older copies of pages that carry no date.

            Tell us if we are wrong. The button at the bottom right of this page sends us a note, and a correction that carries the address of the document is the most useful thing anyone can send.

            How to read the scores

            Publishing a constitution and covering models with it are scored separately. Alibaba's constitution names no model, and on a single "has a constitution" column it would score the same as one that binds a company's products.

            The rows say more than the figure. OpenAI and Anthropic are the two highest on what is published and fall short in opposite ways. OpenAI keeps an archive of its versions, one short of complete, where Anthropic keeps none. Anthropic names the things its models must never do as a closed set, where OpenAI's top level points at detailed policies it does not publish.

            The two figures do not agree with each other. Meta publishes almost nothing and is third on what it engages, which leaves it fourth on the final score. Mistral AI is fifth on what is published and scores 0 on what it engages, the only company that does, which leaves it eighth. That is why both figures stay on the board under the final score.

            Six of the nine companies publish no constitution. Most of what the board asks about published rules cannot apply to them, which is why they score so little on the first figure. Among the three that do publish, the change log is what separates them.

            Who the companies are

            Some trading names mislead, so here are the companies behind three of them. Moonshot AI is Beijing Moonshot AI Technology (北京月之暗面科技有限公司), which makes the Kimi models. DeepSeek is 杭州深度求索人工智能基础技术研究有限公司, of Hangzhou, spun out of the High-Flyer (幻方) quantitative investment fund. Mistral AI is Mistral AI SAS, of Paris.

            Limitations

            Both working papers were written to argue for change, and scoring them gives them more precision than they were designed for. The descriptions of each score are ours, and a different reading could move a company by a few points. The wider picture would stay: all nine fall short on the change log, none offers a comment window before one of the things a model must never do is weakened, and none covers government, defence and other special deployments.

            Every score rests on a document found and dated, in the reading of 18 to 21 September 2026. These points are open.

            • Alibaba's constitution serves no text at its own address. A plain request to it returns an application shell and no document, so the text used is the copy this index holds, 210 passages of it, word for word, at its April 2026 version. The index keeps only passages that bear on a behaviour, so prefaces and boilerplate are not in it. The preface's invitation to public comment and the overview's acknowledgement of safeguards the service applies around the model are both confirmed, and there is no statement of coverage anywhere in the document. Anyone who tries to check a quotation at that address will meet the same shell.
            • No older copies of the undated pages were found. Where a page carries no date and no earlier copy, a quiet edit to it would not show. This matters most for Google, where three of the relevant documents carry no date at all, and for DeepSeek, whose disclosure page has no earlier copy anyone could find.
            • The Chinese regulatory filings were not found. The Cyberspace Administration of China publishes its lists as attachments that could not be retrieved. So when this board says Moonshot AI publishes no filing number, it means none appears on Moonshot AI's own sites.
            • Whether Anthropic has signed the EU Code of Practice is unconfirmed. Its July 2025 post says it "intends to sign"; its own page of voluntary commitments does not list it. No score depends on this.
            • Which chapters of the EU Code of Practice Mistral has signed is unconfirmed. The European Commission's list names Mistral with no exception, and sets out chapters only for xAI. No score depends on this.
            • Some details of Claude Mythos rest partly on second-hand accounts. That no constitution has been published for it is confirmed from Anthropic's own pages.
            • What Meta's log of updates to its Community Standards contains could not be established, so the reading that it does not cover AI is an inference nobody has checked.
            • The release date of Kimi K3 is disputed between Moonshot AI's own blog (27 July 2026, for the downloadable model) and the UK AI Security Institute and Wikipedia (16 July 2026). No score depends on this.
            • All scores are ours. None comes from an established, published scoring method.

            What comes next

            The scores will be revisited as companies publish, and a correction that carries the address of a document is the most useful thing a reader can send. The four questions come from a working paper that is still being written, so they may change with it.

            The next view, how constitutions are regulated, follows the same documents into law: which jurisdictions require a constitution to be described, to whom, and whether any requires it to be published or its changes to be recorded.
