    What the ranking shows

    How to read this ranking

    Having a model behaviour specification and covering something are scored apart. Alibaba shows why. On a single "has a specification" column, a specification that names no model would score the same as one that binds a company's products, and the two are not the same thing.

    Read the checks, not only the total. The totals put OpenAI and Anthropic one point apart, but they fall short in opposite ways. OpenAI keeps a record of its versions and does not list its hard constraints; Anthropic lists its hard constraints and keeps no record of its versions. A single number hides the most useful thing in the comparison.

    Companies whose models anyone can download are marked, not only ranked. Mistral AI, Moonshot AI and DeepSeek take three of the four lowest places, and a reader who sees only the totals would conclude they are the least responsible companies. What the evidence shows is that these questions have no way to reach a model once it has been downloaded. So the table marks them "Open weights", which is more honest than a low score alone.

    The bottom of the ranking comes down to the first question. Six of the nine companies publish no model behaviour specification, so most of the grid cannot apply to them, and they sit at the bottom for that one reason. To compare the companies that do publish, look at the change log. To see the case for publishing at all, look at the totals.

    A caveat. The four questions were written to argue for change, not to measure. Turning them into a scored grid gives them a precision they were not designed for, and the descriptions of each score are ours. Anyone could move a company by a few points by reading a description differently. What does not move is the overall picture: all nine fall short on the change log, none offers a comment window before weakening a hard constraint, and none covers government, defence and other special deployments.

    How we scored

    The four questions are the four asks of a transparency proposal Polaris Collective has been drafting since 10 September 2026. Each is split into checks scored from 0 to 4. The first two questions have three checks each, because together they are the minimum the proposal asks for; the other two have two. The total is out of 40.

    The tables below say what earns 0, 2 and 4 on each check. A score of 1 or 3 falls between the descriptions either side of it.

    Supporting practices

    Five further practices, taken from Kembery and colleagues, Emerging International Best Practices for AI Model Specs, keeping only those an outsider can check from public sources. Each is scored 0, 1 or 2, for a total out of 10. They are reported beside the ranking and not counted in it, because they measure neighbouring good practice rather than how changes are governed.

      What we could not check

      Researched on 18 September 2026 from primary sources, with one search for evidence per company. Every score rests on a document we found and dated. These points are not yet confirmed.

      • Alibaba's model behaviour specification was not read at its own address. The page at s.alibaba.com/aaig/specification is built in the browser, and our tools could not retrieve it. The text we used comes from this index, which holds 210 passages of the document, word for word, at its April 2026 version. The index keeps only passages that bear on a behaviour, so it skips prefaces and boilerplate. Two things reported elsewhere as part of the preface, an invitation to public comment and an acknowledgement of safeguards applied by the service around the model, are neither confirmed nor ruled out. A statement of coverage in the preface could change Alibaba's score on check 1.2. The page has yet to be read in a browser.
      • Archived copies could not be checked. The Internet Archive (web.archive.org) could not be reached throughout, so we could not detect silent edits to any undated page. This matters most for Google, where three of the relevant documents carry no date at all, and for DeepSeek, whose disclosure page has never been archived there.
      • Chinese regulatory filings could not be listed. The Cyberspace Administration of China publishes its lists of filings as attachments we could not retrieve. So when we say Moonshot AI publishes no filing number, we mean we could not find one on its own sites, not that none exists.
      • Whether Anthropic has signed the EU Code of Practice is unconfirmed. Its July 2025 post says it "intends to sign"; its own page of voluntary commitments does not list it. No score depends on this.
      • Which chapters of the EU Code of Practice Mistral has signed is unconfirmed. The European Commission's list names Mistral with no exception, and sets out chapters only for xAI. No score depends on this.
      • Some details of Claude Mythos rest partly on secondary sources, though the absence of a published model behaviour specification for it is confirmed from Anthropic's own pages.
      • Meta's log of updates to its Community Standards could not be read, because it too is built in the browser, so our view that it does not cover AI is an inference rather than a check.
      • The release date of Kimi K3 is disputed between Moonshot AI's own blog (27 July 2026, for the downloadable model) and the UK AI Security Institute and Wikipedia (16 July 2026). No score depends on this.
      • All scores are ours. None comes from an established, published scoring method.
