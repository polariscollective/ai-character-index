What each constitution says, and how well it is built

AI companies publish constitutions: documents setting out how their models should* behave. The index reads them and scores each one twice, out of 10 each, and the board leads with the two added together.

The document as a whole. Whether the constitution settles a clash between two of its own rules, whether a reader can tell which rules are absolute and which can be lifted and by whom, whether the rules come with reasons, whether they reach the situations the models are used in, and whether the document contradicts itself somewhere without saying which rule wins.

The behaviours. How far the constitution goes on each behaviour the index asks about, from saying nothing about it to setting rules and showing them applied to worked examples.

Missing a constitution, or a behaviour the index should be asking about? Propose one.

    How each company governs the rules its models follow

    Some AI companies publish a constitution, a document setting out how their models should* behave. This view scores nine companies, as of September 2026, on two things side by side: the rules they publish, and whether those rules are made to bind the models.

    What is published, out of 10. Whether the company publishes a constitution and says which models it governs, whether every change to the rules a model follows can be seen, which filters it runs around its models, whether the public gets a say before one of the things its model must never do is weakened, and whether the published text may be reused. This is the figure the companies are ranked by.

    What it engages, out of 10. Whether the document binds the models: published tests of adherence, outside evaluators, a threshold before release, who may approve a change, training, internal use and monitoring. It sits beside the rank and does not enter it.

    The two are never added. The checks are anchored line by line and the practices are not, so a single total would read as more precise than the scores behind it. One further practice is scored for nobody, because only an internal audit could show it, and every company is marked NA on it.

    How the scoring works

    Where the rows come from

    The board has two sources and no others. The four questions are the four asks of Polaris Collective's working paper "Model spec governance and transparency", in its draft of 10 September 2026. The ten best practices beside them come from "Emerging International Best Practices for AI Model Specs", a working paper by Edward Kembery and colleagues that is under review. Every row on the board says which of the two it comes from, under its name, and the paper's own words sit in the popover the row's name opens. Neither paper splits a question into checks, weighs one row against another, or says what earns a score. We did all three, and every score on the board is ours.

    How a figure is worked out

    A question is split into checks, each scored from 0 to 4: three for each of the first two questions and two for the others. Every check carries a written description of what earns 0, what earns 2 and what earns 4, in the tables below. A question's own figure is the average of its checks. A best practice is scored 0, 1 or 2 against one scale that covers all of them.

    Each row belongs to one figure and one only. A figure is the mean of its rows, each taken as a share of its own maximum, times ten. What is published has eleven rows, the ten checks and the licence practice, so every check counts the same and a question with three checks weighs more than one with two. What it engages has eight, all of them practices.

    The two figures are never added. The checks are anchored line by line; the practices share one generic scale with no line of their own. A single total would give a precision the scores behind it do not have. Adding them would mean putting the practices on the same anchored scale and scoring the nine companies again, which is separate work and has not been done.

    Companies are ranked on what is published. The second figure never breaks a tie, so companies level on the first share a place.

    The first two questions are what our working paper calls its minimum, the asks it expects of every company before the other two: a published constitution for every model in use, and one public change log. The tables below say what earns 0, 2 and 4 on each check. A score of 1 or 3 falls between the descriptions either side of it.

    The best practices, figure by figure

    One practice is counted in what is published, because it is a fact about the published text.

      Four are counted in what it engages and can be checked by anyone from public sources.

        Four more are counted in the same figure, and nobody outside a company can see whether it follows them. The paper asks companies to say so publicly, so each is scored 0, 1 or 2 on what the company publishes, and 0 when it publishes nothing.

          One is scored for nobody. The paper raises it as an open problem and does not ask companies to publish it, only an internal audit could show it, no such audit has been done, and every company is marked NA on it. It is counted in neither figure.

            The paper gives no description of what 0, 1 and 2 mean on a practice. In our scoring, 2 is the practice in full, 1 part of it or a weaker form of it, and 0 means we found nothing published.

            How we looked

            Every score was given by hand, by reading documents. No model judged any row, and no score was taken from another ranking. The reading was done between 18 and 21 September 2026, and the sources it rests on are listed company by company below.

            For each company we searched its own pages first: the constitution or policy document where one exists, the developer documentation, the model cards and system cards, the terms of service and usage policy, the change logs and release notes, and the safety or frontier framework. We then searched for the company by name against the practices it had not answered, and read the outside sources listed below, among them the EU Code of Practice signatory list, the Stanford Foundation Model Transparency Index, and the evaluations published by the UK AI Security Institute and the US Center for AI Standards and Innovation. For the four practices only a company can show, the search was run one practice at a time across all nine companies, so that each practice was judged the same way everywhere.

            Where nothing is published, the board says nothing is published. A 0 is not a judgement that a company does not do something. It says we looked and found nothing public, and a company may well follow a practice without saying so. Every score above 0 on the four practices only a company can show rests on a passage the company published, quoted with its address in the popover behind that score. Two searches failed outright, and both are named under what could not be established: the Chinese regulatory filings, and older copies of pages that carry no date.

            Tell us if we are wrong. The button at the bottom right of this page sends us a note, and a correction that carries the address of the document is the most useful thing anyone can send.

            How to read the scores

            Publishing a constitution and covering models with it are scored separately. Alibaba's constitution names no model, and on a single "has a constitution" column it would score the same as one that binds a company's products.

            The rows say more than the figure. OpenAI and Anthropic are the two highest on what is published and fall short in opposite ways. OpenAI keeps a record of its versions and does not list the things its models must never do, while Anthropic lists them and keeps no record of its versions.

            The two figures do not agree with each other. Meta publishes almost nothing and is third on what it engages. Mistral AI is fifth on what is published and scores 0 on what it engages, the only company that does. That is why the two are shown side by side rather than merged.

            Companies whose models anyone can download are marked. Mistral AI, Moonshot AI and DeepSeek take three of the five lowest places on what is published. These questions have no way to reach a model once it has been downloaded, so the table marks these companies "Open weights".

            Six of the nine companies publish no constitution. Most of what the board asks about published rules cannot apply to them, which is why they score so little on the first figure. Among the three that do publish, the change log is what separates them.

            Who the companies are

            Some trading names mislead, so here are the companies behind three of them. Moonshot AI is Beijing Moonshot AI Technology (北京月之暗面科技有限公司), which makes the Kimi models. DeepSeek is 杭州深度求索人工智能基础技术研究有限公司, of Hangzhou, spun out of the High-Flyer (幻方) quantitative investment fund. Mistral AI is Mistral AI SAS, of Paris.

            What could not be established

            Both working papers were written to argue for change, and scoring them gives them more precision than they were designed for. The descriptions of each score are ours, and a different reading could move a company by a few points. The wider picture would stay: all nine fall short on the change log, none offers a comment window before one of the things a model must never do is weakened, and none covers government, defence and other special deployments.

            Every score rests on a document found and dated, in the reading of 18 to 21 September 2026. These points are open.

            • Alibaba's constitution was not read at its own address. The text used is the copy this index holds, 210 passages of it, word for word, at its April 2026 version. The index keeps only passages that bear on a behaviour, so prefaces and boilerplate are not in it. Two things reported elsewhere as part of the preface, an invitation to public comment and an acknowledgement of safeguards the service applies around the model, are neither confirmed nor ruled out. A statement of coverage in the preface would change Alibaba's score on check 1.2.
            • No older copies of the undated pages were found. Where a page carries no date and no earlier copy, a quiet edit to it would not show. This matters most for Google, where three of the relevant documents carry no date at all, and for DeepSeek, whose disclosure page has no earlier copy anyone could find.
            • The Chinese regulatory filings were not found. The Cyberspace Administration of China publishes its lists as attachments that could not be retrieved. So when this board says Moonshot AI publishes no filing number, it means none appears on Moonshot AI's own sites.
            • Whether Anthropic has signed the EU Code of Practice is unconfirmed. Its July 2025 post says it "intends to sign"; its own page of voluntary commitments does not list it. No score depends on this.
            • Which chapters of the EU Code of Practice Mistral has signed is unconfirmed. The European Commission's list names Mistral with no exception, and sets out chapters only for xAI. No score depends on this.
            • Some details of Claude Mythos rest partly on second-hand accounts. That no constitution has been published for it is confirmed from Anthropic's own pages.
            • What Meta's log of updates to its Community Standards contains could not be established, so the reading that it does not cover AI is an inference nobody has checked.
            • The release date of Kimi K3 is disputed between Moonshot AI's own blog (27 July 2026, for the downloadable model) and the UK AI Security Institute and Wikipedia (16 July 2026). No score depends on this.
            • All scores are ours. None comes from an established, published scoring method.
