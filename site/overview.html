What each constitution says, and how well it is built

AI companies publish constitutions: documents setting out how their models should* behave. The index reads them and scores each one twice, out of 10 each, and the board leads with the two added together.

The document as a whole. Whether the constitution settles a clash between two of its own rules, whether a reader can tell which rules are absolute and which can be lifted and by whom, whether the rules come with reasons, whether they reach the situations the models are used in, and whether the document contradicts itself somewhere without saying which rule wins.

The behaviours. How far the constitution goes on each behaviour the index asks about, from saying nothing about it to setting rules and showing them applied to worked examples.

Missing a constitution, or a behaviour the index should be asking about? Propose one.

    How each company governs the rules its models follow

    Some AI companies publish a constitution, a document setting out how their models should* behave. This view asks four questions of nine companies, as of September 2026, and scores the answers out of 4 each.

    The four questions, out of 16 together. Whether the company publishes a constitution and says which models it governs, whether every change to the rules a model follows can be seen, which filters it runs around its models, and whether the public gets a say before one of the things its model must never do is weakened.

    How the scoring works

    The four questions

    The four questions are the four asks of Polaris Collective's working paper, in its draft of 10 September 2026. Each is split into checks scored from 0 to 4: three for each of the first two questions and two for the others. The first two are what the paper calls its minimum, the asks it expects of every company before the other two: a published constitution for every model in use, and one public change log. A question's score is the average of its checks, and the overall score is the sum of the four questions, out of 16, so every question weighs the same.

    The tables below say what earns 0, 2 and 4 on each check. A score of 1 or 3 falls between the descriptions either side of it.

    Best practices, outside the total

    Five further practices, from Kembery et al., Emerging International Best Practices for AI Model Specs, keeping only those anyone can check from public sources. Each is scored 0, 1 or 2, for a total out of 10. They are shown beside the ranking and left out of its total, because they measure related good practice. The paper gives no description of each score. In our scoring, 2 is the practice in full, 1 part of it or a weaker form of it, and 0 means we found nothing.

      Best practices only the company can show

      Four more practices from the same paper. Nobody outside a company can see whether it follows them, and the paper asks companies to say so publicly. Each is scored 0, 1 or 2 on what the company publishes, and 0 when it publishes nothing. A company may well follow a practice without saying so: where we score 0, we found nothing published and no audit anyone can verify. Tell us if we are wrong.

        A best practice only an internal audit could score

        One more practice from the same paper, which it raises as an open problem and does not ask companies to publish. Only an internal audit could show it. No such audit has been done, every company is marked NA, not assessed, and it counts towards nothing.

          How to read the scores

          Publishing a constitution and covering models with it are scored separately. Alibaba's constitution names no model, and on a single "has a constitution" column it would score the same as one that binds a company's products.

          The checks say more than the overall score. OpenAI and Anthropic have the same overall score and fall short in opposite ways. OpenAI keeps a record of its versions and does not list the things its models must never do, while Anthropic lists them and keeps no record of its versions.

          Companies whose models anyone can download are marked. Mistral AI, Moonshot AI and DeepSeek take three of the five lowest places. These questions have no way to reach a model once it has been downloaded, so the table marks these companies "Open weights".

          The bottom of the ranking comes down to the first question. Six of the nine companies publish no constitution, so most of the grid cannot apply to them and they sit at the bottom for that reason. Among the companies that do publish, the change log is what separates them.

          Who the companies are

          Some trading names mislead, so here are the companies behind three of them. Moonshot AI is Beijing Moonshot AI Technology (北京月之暗面科技有限公司), which makes the Kimi models. DeepSeek is 杭州深度求索人工智能基础技术研究有限公司, of Hangzhou, spun out of the High-Flyer (幻方) quantitative investment fund. Mistral AI is Mistral AI SAS, of Paris.

          What could not be established

          The four questions were written to argue for change, and scoring them gives them more precision than they were designed for. The descriptions of each score are ours, and a different reading could move a company by a few points. The wider picture would stay: all nine fall short on the change log, none offers a comment window before one of the things a model must never do is weakened, and none covers government, defence and other special deployments.

          Every score rests on a document found and dated, as of 18 September 2026. These points are open.

          • Alibaba's constitution was not read at its own address. The text used is the copy this index holds, 210 passages of it, word for word, at its April 2026 version. The index keeps only passages that bear on a behaviour, so prefaces and boilerplate are not in it. Two things reported elsewhere as part of the preface, an invitation to public comment and an acknowledgement of safeguards the service applies around the model, are neither confirmed nor ruled out. A statement of coverage in the preface would change Alibaba's score on check 1.2.
          • No older copies of the undated pages were found. Where a page carries no date and no earlier copy, a quiet edit to it would not show. This matters most for Google, where three of the relevant documents carry no date at all, and for DeepSeek, whose disclosure page has no earlier copy anyone could find.
          • The Chinese regulatory filings were not found. The Cyberspace Administration of China publishes its lists as attachments that could not be retrieved. So when this board says Moonshot AI publishes no filing number, it means none appears on Moonshot AI's own sites.
          • Whether Anthropic has signed the EU Code of Practice is unconfirmed. Its July 2025 post says it "intends to sign"; its own page of voluntary commitments does not list it. No score depends on this.
          • Which chapters of the EU Code of Practice Mistral has signed is unconfirmed. The European Commission's list names Mistral with no exception, and sets out chapters only for xAI. No score depends on this.
          • Some details of Claude Mythos rest partly on second-hand accounts. That no constitution has been published for it is confirmed from Anthropic's own pages.
          • What Meta's log of updates to its Community Standards contains could not be established, so the reading that it does not cover AI is an inference nobody has checked.
          • The release date of Kimi K3 is disputed between Moonshot AI's own blog (27 July 2026, for the downloadable model) and the UK AI Security Institute and Wikipedia (16 July 2026). No score depends on this.
          • All scores are ours. None comes from an established, published scoring method.
